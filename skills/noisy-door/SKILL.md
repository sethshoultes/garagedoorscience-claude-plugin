# A noisy garage door

Apply the `stay-safe` skill first and keep it on the whole conversation. Talk to one person, in plain words, one question at a time. Start with the sound: is it a grind, a rattle, a squeak or a bang, when does it happen (going up, coming down, or both), and is it the door or the opener that is loud.

If the Garage Door Science connector is available, use it for the symptom too, and tell the homeowner when you are doing that. Do not invent causes or numbers. If you do not know, say so.

## A loud bang, then the door will not lift

Stop. Do not touch the door and do not try to open it. A bang from the spring area above the door can mean a broken spring, and that is a technician's job, always. Go to `find-a-pro`. Do not describe any spring or cable work.

## Grinding

Clopay names two causes: tracks out of line that make the rollers fight their way along, and worn gears in the opener. A homeowner can do one safe check: with the door fully closed, look along the tracks for anything bent, loose or out of line, and look for rollers that have visibly worn or come loose. Do not tell them to replace rollers or to adjust the track: the bottom rollers sit in the brackets at the bottom corners, which are connected to the springs, under extreme tension and a pro's job. A grind that does not go away after the lubrication steps below means a technician should look.

## Rattling

Something has worked loose. A homeowner can look, from the floor with the door closed, at the track brackets and the hinges for bolts that are loose or missing, and write down where. Do not tell them to tighten anything: Chamberlain says not to adjust brackets or other door hardware yourself, and the bottom brackets are under extreme tension. Tell them to give the technician the list. A rattle from the opener itself (a loose chain slapping the rail) is covered in the opener's manual, so point them to the manual rather than giving a turn count.

## Squeaking

Squeaks are usually dry metal. This is the one noise a homeowner can usually fix. These are the rules our lubrication article uses:

- Lubricate the hinges, steel rollers and bearings. Use a spray made for garage doors, the type the door maker's manual names (Wayne Dalton and Amarr say non-silicone), sparingly, and wipe off the excess.
- Never on nylon rollers, and never on the tracks (wipe the tracks with a damp cloth instead).
- Clopay says to avoid WD-40 and oil-based products, which it says draw dirt and dust.
- Every three to six months, closer to three for a door that gets heavy use.
- Do not touch the spring or anything attached to it. We leave that to a technician. Do not tell anyone to lubricate a spring.

## The opener is the noisy part

Say that the noise may be in the opener, not the door. Ask the homeowner to stand with the door closed, press the button, and say whether the sound comes from the motor housing on the ceiling or from the door and tracks. If it is the opener, the manual covers its adjustments and a technician can say whether it is worth repairing. Do not tell anyone to open the opener's housing, and do not tell them to lift the door by hand to test it.

## When it does not fit

Say you are not sure, ask one more question, and offer `find-a-pro`. Never guess at a spring, cable, track or bracket problem.
